01 — Theory

Theorising Science Diplomacy in the Age of AI

Science diplomacy plays a growing role in addressing major global challenges with a scientific dimension, whether climate change, pandemics or artificial intelligence (AI). A thorough understanding of the concept is therefore essential to grasp its full scope and harness its potential. The taxonomy proposed in 2010 by the Royal Society and the AAAS, distinguishing science for diplomacy, diplomacy for science, and science in diplomacy, remains an essential reference for thinking about these interactions, but its limitations call for further theoretical discussion. The Chair therefore proposes to deepen the theoretical foundations of the concept, explore its dual nature (as a factor of both cooperation and competition between States) and identify the conditions under which it can be operationalised in the specific context of AI.

02 — Governance

Rethinking Global AI Governance

Global AI governance refers to the full range of initiatives — scientific, normative, and institutional — that aim to limit transnational AI risks and amplify potential benefits. Global AI governance has passed through two periods: a first centred on ethics (OECD Principles, UNESCO Recommendation), followed by a second oriented toward standards and binding legal instruments (EU AI Act, Council of Europe Framework Convention). A third period has since opened, marked by resurgent national interests and a waning commitment to binding international norms. In this sense, science diplomacy can then play a strategic role by supporting the implementation of existing norms through the mobilisation of scientific expertise, and by contributing to the design of governance initiatives that treat science as a resource of power. The Chair will produce a living map of existing initiatives as well as comparative analyses aimed at identifying the configurations in which science diplomacy is deployed, or could be deployed further.

03 — Tools

Mobilising AI in the Service of Science Diplomacy Actors

AI is now among the tools diplomats draw on in carrying out their duties. By making it possible to analyse vast data sets in real time, produce predictive models, or generate strategies and draft resolutions and reports, AI allows diplomats to be better informed and to make better use of their time, freeing them from certain bureaucratic and administrative tasks in favour of human contact. These contributions are not, however, without risk: both the diplomatic function itself and the goal of reducing inequalities in diplomacy between States may be affected by diplomats’ use of AI. It is therefore essential to reflect on the responsible use of AI in this context, which has led the Chair to conduct research on this question and to develop tools for diplomats.
